---
title: "Statement of Purpose"
---
During one of my undergraduate neuroscience classes, a visiting neurosurgeon played a video of a patient with a tremor undergoing surgery to get deep brain stimulation electrodes implanted. The patient was awake and holding a guitar, but unable to play it. With every attempt to pluck a string, the neurosurgeons mapped his motor circuits. Using this functional readout, they were finally able to find the right spot, stimulate it and enable him to play the guitar seamlessly! The impact of this intervention stayed with me because it highlighted the importance of returning to patients the abilities they might have lost. In the clinic I gained further insights into the programming required for optimal symptom control with these devices. What started as a curiosity to understand the mechanisms by which this therapy worked, soon transformed into a commitment to discovering ways to improve it through scientific inquiry and clinical implementation.

These experiences inspired my doctoral research, which investigated cellular mechanisms to guide deep brain stimulation patterns capable of producing long lasting motor recovery in a preclinical model of Parkinson's disease. Throughout my PhD, I never lost sight of the patients. Eventually, our novel stimulation paradigm was tested in patients whose symptoms remain refractory to existing therapies by the same neurosurgeon from my undergraduate class. The impact of therapeutic innovation on patients with complex neurological disorders has formed the foundation of my drive to be a physician-scientist.

My interest in improving neurological diagnosis and treatment began during undergraduate research, where I developed a machine learning-based seizure detection algorithm for intracranial EEG. This experience taught me to think simultaneously as a scientist and clinician, translating neural circuit biology and the realities of patient care into meaningful solutions. Since then, I have pursued neurological questions across multiple scales, from EEG network analysis and diffusion tractography research at the NIH to investigating thalamic subcircuits underlying tremor and bradykinesia during my PhD. More than a decade of neuroscience research has strengthened my technical and analytical skills, but more importantly, it has taught me to embrace uncertainty, think systematically, and value the process of solving complex problems.

Although my research was motivated by Parkinson's disease, my neurology rotations revealed a broader passion for caring for patients across the full spectrum of neurological disorders. During my neurology sub-internship, I encountered three patients with similar cranial nerve deficits whose final diagnoses proved to be neurosarcoidosis, Lyme neuroborreliosis, and Miller-Fisher syndrome. Diagnosing these patients required developing broad differential diagnoses and refining them through careful neurological examination and targeted testing. The process felt remarkably familiar. Forming hypotheses, gathering evidence, and applying that knowledge to guide optimal treatment is the same approach I have spent the last decade developing as a researcher. Recognizing this overlap confirmed that neurology is the right specialty for me.

I look forward to building the clinical expertise necessary to care for patients with neurological disease while continuing to pursue questions in therapeutic optimization and mechanistic discovery. I hope to train in a program that values both scientific innovation and compassionate patient care, allowing me to advance the field while providing exceptional care to patients facing complex neurological conditions.
