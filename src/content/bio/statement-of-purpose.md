---
title: "Statement of Purpose"
---
I witnessed firsthand the impact of therapeutic innovation on patients with complex neurological disorders, from epilepsy to movement disorders. Powerful clinical moments like witnessing a tremor fade in the operating room or gait improving after adjusting a patient's implanted device settings in clinic have both inspired and driven my desire to scientific inquiry. Such moments inspired my thesis research, which leveraged cellular mechanisms to guide deep brain stimulation patterns capable of restoring motor function, acutely and persistently, in a preclinical model of Parkinson's disease (PD). Throughout my PhD, I never lost sight of the patients this work was meant to serve: those refractory to existing treatments who depend on novel therapies to restore function.

My interest in building tools that optimize diagnosis, and intervention began with my undergraduate research developing a machine-learning-based seizure detection algorithm for intracranial EEG. This work taught me to think simultaneously as a scientist and a clinician, translating the realities of inpatient epilepsy monitoring and neural circuit-level disease mechanisms into usable signal features. Since then, I've approached neurological questions across scales: network-level analysis with EEG, diffusion-based tractography at the NIH, and finally cellular-level investigation of thalamic sub-circuits in a mouse model of tremor and bradykinesia during my thesis. Ten-plus years in neuroscience research have sharpened my suite of technical, engineering, and analytical skills, but more importantly, they've taught me to navigate ambiguity and value process over outcome, a lesson that will serve me well in clinical neurology.

My curiosity for the translation of aberrant neural signals into symptoms informs my clinical decision making. When a patient with a polyneuropathy got an EMG, or a patient with epilepsy was on continuous EEG monitoring, I was as interested in the signals as I was in their clinical correlates. I wanted to uncover subtle clues in these data about why the patient presented the way they did or responded to treatment in a certain manner. I cared deeply about tailoring interventions to individual patient needs, instead of a one-size-fits all approach. This was facilitated by thinking creatively and curiously about each patient.

My thesis research was motivated by patients with Parkinson's disease, but during the neurology rotations I found real passion for direct clinical impact across MS, ALS, movement disorders, headache, and stroke. During my neurology sub-internship, we saw three patients who had very similar presentations, involving predominantly cranial nerve deficits. Interestingly, the diagnosis ended up being different for each case — Neurosarcoidosis, Lyme neuroborreliosis, and Miller-Fisher syndrome. The initial approach to these cases involved a broad differential, refined by deficits noted on the neurological exam that would support certain diagnoses. That process of forming a hypothesis, gathering evidence through exam and workup, and implementing an optimized intervention is the exact process I've spent a decade honing, in my research. Recognizing that overlap confirmed neurology as the right path for me.

I look forward to building my clinical skills to impact patients with neurological disorders in the acute setting, while pursuing longer-term goals in therapeutic optimization and mechanistic discovery. I aspire to join a program that values innovation alongside compassion, so that I can deliver the best possible care to patients who are facing complex neurological conditions.
